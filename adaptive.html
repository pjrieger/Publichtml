Adaptive Cycling Patrol Operations

American River Bike Patrol (ARBP) | Technical & First Response Protocol

  • Objective: Equip patrollers with essential medical, mechanical, and tactical awareness for supporting adaptive cyclists on the American River Parkway.
  • Liaison Role: Technical assistance, rider advocacy, and NSP-aligned field protocols.
  • Format: 10-slide action guide optimized for field reference.

1. Introduction & Equipment Spectrum

Adaptive cycles are specialized mobility devices engineered for specific rider physical profiles:

  • Handcycles: Hand-crank driven, 3-wheel configuration (lower-body paralysis or amputation).
  • Recumbent Trikes: Seated position, lower center of gravity (balance, joint, or neurological support).
  • e-Assist Modules: Hub or mid-drive motor augmentation (vital for fatigue and cardiac management).

2. Trail Clearance & Geometry

Recognize physical limits of adaptive equipment on parkway trails:

  • Width: Recumbents/handcycles are wider (up to 36"+)—require extra passing buffer.
  • Low Clearance: Derailleurs and hand-crank chainrings sit inches off the ground. Highly vulnerable to root strikes and gravel debris.
  • Turning Radius: Wider turning arc; cannot pivot sharply or make rapid evasive maneuvers.

3. Critical Medical Focus: Autonomic Dysreflexia

⚠️ EMERGENCY PROTOCOL (Spinal Cord Injuries T-6 or above)

  • Trigger: Acute response to painful or noxious stimuli below the level of injury (e.g., full bladder, skin pressure, tight straps).
  • Symptoms: Severe sudden headache, rapid high blood pressure, profuse sweating above injury level.
  • Patrol Action: Sit rider upright immediately, locate/remove stimulus (loosen straps/clothing), request immediate ALS.

4. Secondary Medical Considerations

  • Altered Pain Sensation: Rider may not feel fractures, skin abrasions, or pressure sores resulting from a fall.
  • Thermoregulation: Impaired ability to sweat or shiver below injury line; high risk for heat illness or hypothermia.
  • Caregiver Coordination: Always communicate with riding partners or caregivers—they understand the rider’s baseline medical profile best.

5. Trailside Mechanicals: In-Chassis Repair

  • Immobile Riders: Many adaptive riders cannot dismount to allow bike flipping or standard maintenance.
  • In-Chassis Repairs: Fix flats, adjust brakes, and reset chains while the rider remains strapped into the frame.
  • Chain-Line Complexity: Handcycles often use multi-stage chains or jackshafts. Inspect the entire drivetrain run for bound links.

6. Mechanicals: Hydraulics & E-Systems

  • Hydraulic Brakes: Frequent on trikes for high stopping power. Inspect lines for pinch points or fluid leaks after a rollover.
  • E-Assist Diagnostics: Check battery seat connections and handlebar display wiring before assuming motor failure.
  • Manual Release: Know how to disengage motor systems if e-trike power cuts out completely.

7. Triage & Transfer Protocols

  • Do Not Unstrap Unnecessarily: Custom seating harnesses and chest supports stabilize the torso. Do not unstrap unless medically required.
  • Two-Person Assist: If transfer is necessary, assign one patroller for torso/head control and one for legs.
  • Equipment Security: Adaptive bikes are high-value custom medical gear. Coordinate trail clearing/recovery carefully.

8. ARBP Patrol Action Summary

  • 1. Assess: Identify cycle type, rider stability, and presence of spinal cord injuries.
  • 2. Protect: Secure scene; provide shade/warmth (thermoregulation awareness).
  • 3. Triage: Check for AD symptoms or unfelt injuries. Perform in-chassis fixes when possible.
  • 4. Coordinate: Involve riding partner and communicate equipment specifics to dispatch.
